How the model worksin plain words

It's a Monte Carlo simulation: the same war, played out by chance 1,000,000 times, then counted. Every odd in it is our judgment, written down with what informed it. No source gives these numbers, and nobody can measure them.

  1. One war runs in quarter-years, from July 1941 to the end of 1947. The first turn is July 1941: does Japan strike north at the Soviet Union while Hitler's armies are deep in Russia? The second is November 1941: back down to America's terms, or go south, and how: Pearl Harbor and the south (the real plan), only the British and Dutch colonies (road B), or the Philippines without Pearl Harbor. Then a turn every three months. Nothing is forced: every choice reads the state of that war.
  2. Battles are events. 11 of them can happen: Pearl Harbor, the Siberian offensive, the decisive battle at sea, the Indian Ocean, the Coral Sea, Midway, Guadalcanal, Imphal and Kohima, the Philippine Sea, Leyte Gulf, and Iwo Jima and Okinawa. Each is fought only if that war gets there, and each is decided by the forces on each side (carriers times the quality of their pilots, plus land-based aircraft where they count), by intelligence (the side that has read the other's plan strikes first, as America did at Midway) and by luck. The loser loses most of what it committed, all of it when caught by surprise. The losses carry on: a carrier sunk at Midway is missing at Guadalcanal.
  3. The economy decides how long. Oil: Japan starts with 43 million barrels, burns 5.9 million a quarter at full operations, and once the south is taken imports up to 3.6 million a quarter, until American submarines go after the tankers. Shipping: Japan starts with 6 million tons; submarines, aircraft and mines sink a share each quarter at the rates in the Navy's own loss tables, and Japan's yards replace part of it. Production: Japan's output follows its shipping, minus the bombing and the 25% tied down in China. America builds its carriers on the real schedule and 4.5 aircraft for every Japanese one. The American advance takes one step at a time, at a chance set by the strength on each side: Guadalcanal and the Solomons, the Gilberts and Marshalls, New Guinea, the Marianas, the Philippines, Iwo Jima and Okinawa, and the home islands in range.
  4. The talks. Each quarter the strain on Washington is added up from what Japan has done: holding the initiative, a carrier fleet that rivals America's, Americans killed that quarter, Hawaii held, a stalemate dragging on, war weariness. The more strain, the likelier Washington talks peace: for an attacked America, 0.08% a quarter for each unit of strain at the middle odds, up to a cap. Talks stop once America is clearly winning. If Washington talks while Britain is down too (Germany has won in Europe, India is threatened, or Japan holds Hawaii) and London signs, Japan keeps what it took: Japan wins. Otherwise Japan may accept a peace that hands back its conquests: a draw, Japan keeps its pre-war empire (Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria). With America out of the war, Britain and the Netherlands can also give up the East on their own.
  5. The end. Germany falls in spring 1945 (each war draws its own date), the Soviets attack two to three months later, and the bomb is ready in summer 1945 once the Marianas are held. Each quarter Japan may surrender, more likely the closer America has come and with the bombs, the Soviets, or both. Blockade and hunger add to it. If none of that works, America may land in Japan.
  6. One pass is one war. Then we count how each one ended.

What "a draw or better" means

Japan wins: A peace every great power fighting Japan signs, with Japan keeping what it took in 1941–42 (the south, or the Soviet Far East) and its pre-war empire. A draw: A negotiated peace that hands back the 1941–42 conquests but leaves Japan Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria and its own government. A draw or better counts both: 1.52% of the film's 1,000,000 wars. Holding most of what it took at the end of 1947 with no peace signed is "still at war", not a draw.

One leadership, one temperament

In each war Japan's leadership has one appetite for risk, drawn once (its spread is the last odd on the list). It pushes all 15 of Japan's strategic choices the same way: bolder (strike north, attack America, go for Midway and Hawaii) or more cautious (back down). It never changes luck in a battle. Its average is the middle odds.

Restarting from a turning point

To restart from, say, after Midway, the model replays real history up to that date: every roll goes the way it really went (the same attack on Pearl Harbor, the same battles won and lost). From that date on it runs free. Replayed all the way, it ends in Japan's surrender in 1945, every time: that's one of the tests. The film's restarts are 200,000 wars each, seed 11.

Three things the reviewers made us say

  1. America's will after it was attacked is an input, not a result. We took it from the polls of 1941–42. On 23 February 1942, just after Singapore fell, Gallup asked Americans about a peace that left Japan what it had taken: favor 10%, oppose 81%. On 2 March 1942 the National Opinion Research Center found 88% for "an all-out war against the Japs wherever we can possibly attack them" and 1% for withdrawing and making peace. No poll we found in 1942–45 had more than about one American in five for any negotiated peace with Japan. So in the model an attacked America talks very rarely (0.08% a quarter for each unit of strain), and one that went to war without being attacked talks more (0.5%). That gap is our judgment: no poll asked Americans about a war over British and Dutch colonies. It is the biggest swing in the film: restarted after Pearl Harbor, a draw or better comes in one war in 120 for the attacked America and one in 18 for one that wasn't attacked.
  2. The factories decide how long America takes, and whether the war is over by 1948. Cut American production to one-eighth on the road Japan took (the film's test, 200,000 wars) and the war doesn't end in 1945: America has beaten Japan by the end of 1947 in 56% of wars, and 42% are still going. What stays small is America giving up: a draw or better in 2.1%.
  3. Japanese battle wins can end some wars earlier, so the model does not say they save American lives. Force a Japanese win at Midway and the median American toll in the model is about 89,700, against 91,200 when America wins it; at Guadalcanal 89,600 against 90,900. That isn't a finding. In the island-hopping a Japanese win can stall the advance, and because the bomb and the Soviet entry keep their dates, some wars end before the costliest steps are reached. Where a Japanese win simply makes the war longer, the toll goes up: Kurita pressing on at Leyte, 97,600 against 90,700.

The same answer on every computer

Each war's dice come from the seed and the war's number, nothing else. War 4,488 of seed 19411207 is the same war on any computer, however many cores run it and in whatever order. Seed 19411207, the middle odds and 1,000,000 wars give the film's numbers exactly. War numbers start at 1; in the code the first war is number 0, so war 4,488 is index 4,487 there.

The famous what-ifs22 of them, real and modelled

The ones viewers ask about most. For each: what really happened, from the sources behind the model, and what the model does with it.

"Japan never had a chance"

RealThe US Strategic Bombing Survey (1946) put Japan's economy at about a tenth of America's, able to "support a short war"; once the first blow failed to bring peace, Japan, "without significant help from Germany, was doomed."

The modelMostly agrees, and says how much: Japan wins 0.22% of the film's million and gets a draw or better in 1.52%. Not zero, though: 1,509 of the 2,233 wins come from wars America stays out of or leaves, and Britain and the Netherlands give up the East.

The Hull note

RealOn 26 November 1941 Hull's note asked Japan to "withdraw all military, naval, air and police forces from China and from Indochina". The three-month truce (a modus vivendi) Washington had drafted was dropped the same day: Hull cited China's opposition and 'either the half-hearted support or the actual opposition' of the British, Dutch and Australian governments.

The modelThe odd that America keeps the truce instead is 8%, and then Japan waits a quarter. The odd that Japan backs down to America's terms is 3%, tripled after a truce: both sides' minimum terms on China never overlapped in any document we read. Forced, the truce gives a draw or better in 1.36% (nothing forced: 1.49%).

The Emperor and the surrender

RealOn 9 August 1945 the Big Six split three to three over the Potsdam terms; the Emperor broke the tie that night and again on 14 August. Officers tried a coup on the night of 14–15 August; it failed because the senior army leaders didn't join it.

The modelSurrender is a roll each quarter once America is close: 40% with the atomic bombs alone, 35% with the Soviet attack alone, 80% with both. The Emperor and the coup are inside those odds, not separate ones. Take away both shocks and America lands in Japan in 50% of wars.

The Philippines

RealJapan's planners rejected bypassing the Philippines: American forces there "could possibly disrupt our line of operations", and if the attack waited, "these islands would become impregnable" (Japanese Monograph 150). Corregidor held out until 6 May 1942, and the last organized forces in the islands surrendered by 9 June.

The modelJapan attacks the Philippines but not Pearl Harbor in 3.9% of the film's wars (its odd is 5%, rolled when Japan hasn't chosen road B). On road B, American bombers in the Philippines sit on the flank and cut Japan's oil imports until the islands fall. Forced, the Philippines without Pearl Harbor gives a draw or better in 0.99%.

Port Moresby and the Coral Sea

RealAt the Coral Sea (4–8 May 1942) Lexington and the light carrier Shoho were sunk and Shokaku damaged; the invasion of Port Moresby turned back, and it never fell. The US Strategic Bombing Survey: "for the first time, the Japanese advance had been checked."

The modelThe odd that Japan sends the invasion is 80%. It wins the carrier battle in 38% of those fought, and the odd that the invasion then goes on is 55%. Forced, Port Moresby taken gives a draw or better in 1.21%.

Yamato and the battleships

RealThe super-battleships Yamato and Musashi were built over the air-power advocates' objections. Musashi was sunk by carrier aircraft in the Sibuyan Sea in October 1944; Yamato on 7 April 1945 by "five bomb and ten torpedo hits". The third hull, Shinano, became a carrier only after Midway.

The modelThe odd that Japan builds carriers instead is 10%, and then its carrier output grows. Forced, carriers instead of battleships give a draw or better in 0.94%: by 1943–44 Japan was short of trained pilots more than of decks.

The war in China

RealIn autumn 1941, 28 of Japan's 51 divisions were in China, 13 in Manchuria and Korea and 10 at home (Reports of General MacArthur); only 11 could be given to the whole southern conquest (Morton, Strategy and Command). Tojo brought down a cabinet rather than withdraw. About 1.1 million soldiers were deployed in China during the war.

The modelChina is a drain: 25% of Japan's output tied down there, and a peace with Chiang (an odd of 0.5% a quarter in 1942–43) frees most of it. Forced, peace with China in 1942 gives a draw or better in 0.98%. China's own war (its offensives, Ichi-Go, the Burma Road) isn't modelled.

The Indian Ocean

RealIn April 1942 Nagumo's carriers raided Ceylon and sank the carrier Hermes, two heavy cruisers and other ships, but never found the main Eastern Fleet, and no invasion followed. Germany urged Japan west; the navy "was apathetic" (Hata). Madagascar "was never considered by the Japanese" (the British official history).

The modelThe odd that the carriers go west is 5%, doubled with America out of the war. Japan wins that battle in 93% of those fought, and then India is threatened, so a peace can leave Japan its conquests. It is the road the film's climax, war 4,488, takes. Forced, it gives a draw or better in 0.93%.

The Army and the Navy

RealThe Army wanted to take the British and Dutch colonies first "so as to delay the outbreak of war with the United States as long as possible"; the Navy wanted the Philippines first; the plan adopted did both at once (Japanese Monograph 150). During the Solomons campaign the Army refused to release its pilots from Manchuria and China (a postwar interrogation).

The modelNot modelled as a rivalry. Japan's leadership has one appetite for risk in each war, which pushes all its strategic choices together. The Army's plan is road B, taken in 14.2% of the film's wars.

Balloon bombs and secret weapons

RealFrom November 1944 to April 1945 Japan launched over 9,000 bomb-carrying balloons at North America; the only deaths were six people, a pregnant woman and five children, near Bly, Oregon, on 5 May 1945. Its atomic research never came close to a bomb. Its giant I-400 submarine aircraft carriers never struck; a floatplane from the smaller submarine I-25 firebombed Oregon's forests twice in September 1942, to almost no effect.

The modelNot modelled: none of them could change a battle, the economy or America's will.

"Roosevelt knew"

RealNothing in the record we read shows Washington knew Pearl Harbor would be the target. On 25 November 1941 Stimson wrote that the question was how to "maneuver them into the position of firing the first shot"; most historians (Butow) read it as about a Japanese attack on British, Dutch or Philippine territory. Enterprise and Lexington were away ferrying planes to Wake and Midway, and Saratoga was on the West Coast; Enterprise was due in that morning and was delayed by weather.

The modelThe carriers' absence is luck in the model. On the morning of the attack, the odd that Enterprise is in port is 30%, Lexington 12%, and the radar warning acted on 5%.

Midway: the Aleutians and the missing carriers

RealYamamoto's plan sent forces to the Aleutians and spread the fleet in widely separated groups; the Aleutians took two carriers. Shokaku (damaged) and Zuikaku (short of aircrew) missed Midway after the Coral Sea.

The modelYamamoto splits his forces in 80% of the Midways he attacks, which weakens his strike; after a drawn Coral Sea, the odd that Shokaku and Zuikaku miss it is 70%. Japan wins 23% of the Midways fought. To try six carriers and no Aleutians, set those two odds to 0% in the simulator.

The late declaration of war

RealJapan's 14-part message was to be handed over at 1 p.m. Washington time, 25 minutes before the bombs fell; it reached Hull at 2:20, after the attack, and it declared nothing: "The note did not declare war" (Hull).

The modelNot modelled: even on time it was 25 minutes' notice in a note that broke off talks. What the model does have is whether Americans were attacked at all: America's will is 1 after an attack and 0.6 in a war it chose (our judgment).

The "decisive battle"

RealThe Navy's pre-war plan was to let the American fleet cross the Pacific and destroy it near home in one great battle (Kantai Kessen). America's 1941 war plan (WPL-46) had its fleet raiding the Marshalls, not racing to the Philippines.

The modelIf Japan spares Pearl Harbor and attacks the Philippines, the American fleet sails west into that battle in 30% of those wars. Forced, with Japan winning it, a draw or better comes in 1.39%.

The carriers at sea: Enterprise and Lexington

RealAll three Pacific carriers were away on 7 December: Enterprise and Lexington ferrying planes to Wake and Midway, Saratoga on the West Coast. Enterprise was due in at 7:30 that morning and was delayed by weather.

The modelOnly Enterprise (30%) and Lexington (12%) can be caught in port in the model. Caught there, on the road Japan took, the war lasts about 2 weeks longer and a draw or better moves from 0.9% to 1.1%: the fleet that won the war was still being built.

Lend-Lease through Vladivostok

RealAbout half of America's aid to the Soviet Union, 8.2 million long tons (47%), crossed the Pacific to Vladivostok and other Soviet Far East ports in Soviet-flagged ships, some 125 of them American ships handed over to the Soviets. Japan, neutral with Moscow under their pact of 13 April 1941, let the traffic pass, so only cargo classed as non-military was carried (Motter, the US Army's official history).

The modelNot modelled as its own odd: the Soviet war is simplified. A strike north (4.3% of the film's wars) is where it would bite, and in the model it only puts off the turn south, under the American embargo.

The Panama Canal

RealRear Admiral Yamaguchi's plan to go on to India, Australia, Hawaii, the US West Coast and Panama was "dismissed as a fantasy" (Hata). From 1944 the navy planned a strike on the canal's Gatun Locks by ten floatplanes from four submarines, including the giant I-400 and I-401; it was ready by June 1945, then dropped after Okinawa fell for a strike on the US fleet at Ulithi, which the surrender stopped. Neither was ever flown.

The modelNot modelled: the model has no landing or strike in the Americas. Hawaii is the farthest it reaches, and the West Coast is never reached in a million wars.

Yamamoto shot down (Operation Vengeance)

RealOn 14 April 1943 American codebreakers read a message giving Yamamoto's flight schedule; on 18 April P-38s of the 339th Fighter Squadron from Guadalcanal shot down his bomber over Bougainville.

The modelNot modelled: the model has no individual commanders, only Japan's leadership's appetite for risk. By then the war's direction was set: restarted after Guadalcanal (January 1943), a draw or better comes in one war in 5,000.

Japanese submarines

RealJapan had about 40–45 operational submarines in December 1941, built to support the fleet: "the fundamental mission is auxiliary to the fleet" (Vice Admiral Miwa). They sank Yorktown, Wasp and Juneau in 1942, and later Liscome Bay and Indianapolis, but more and more were used to supply cut-off garrisons, which Miwa called "senseless".

The modelThe odd that Japan's submarines hunt merchant ships instead is 8%; then they wear on American will a little each quarter. Forced, it gives a draw or better in 0.91%.

What if the fleet had been warned?

RealThe Army's Opana radar saw the first wave coming; the report was taken for a flight of American B-17s. Nimitz wrote in 1965 that a fleet caught at sea by a faster Japanese force could have lost "all of our trained men instead of the 3800-approx. lost at P. H." (his figure; 2,403 Americans died there).

The modelThe odd that the warning is acted on is 5%: then the fleet sorties and is caught at sea. Forced, the warned fleet gives a draw or better in 0.94%.

The Two-Ocean Navy Act: the closing window

RealThe Two-Ocean Navy Act of 19 July 1940 had America's new carriers already ordered before Pearl Harbor. In November 1941 Japan's navy chief Nagano put American naval strength at 10 against Japan's 7.5, 60% of it in the Pacific.

The modelAmerica's carriers come on the real schedule whether or not Japan attacks: a neutral America still builds 60% of them. That's why the window was closing, and why no 1941 choice stops them.

The Doolittle raid

RealOn 18 April 1942 American bombers launched from a carrier hit Japan; the damage was small (the US Navy's historians put the dead at about 87). Japan answered with a punitive campaign in China and kept air units at home. Yamamoto had won agreement for his Midway plan two days before; the raid made Tokyo bring the date forward and swept away the last opposition, and the order followed on 5 May.

The modelNot its own odd: the odd that Yamamoto attacks Midway (75%) carries it.

What it leaves outNorth America, China, politics…
  • A landing in North America. None is in the model and none is reachable: Japan's shipping couldn't even keep Hawaii fed (a January 1942 staff study). Hawaii is the farthest it goes.
  • China's own war. China is a drain on Japan (25% of its output) and a rare peace. No Chinese offensives, no Ichi-Go, no Burma Road, no Hump.
  • Japan's internal politics. One appetite for risk per war stands in for the leadership. No cabinets falling, no Army-Navy rivalry, no coup; the Emperor is inside the surrender odds.
  • Most of the Soviet war. A strike north is one offensive, a counter-offensive and the Far East's fate if the USSR is beaten in Europe. Lend-Lease through Vladivostok isn't in it.
  • Polls nobody took. America's will comes from the polls of 1941–42, carried forward. No poll covers a Japanese-held Hawaii, years of stalemate or a war over British colonies, so those strains are our judgment (the talks cap is tested from 4 to 12).
  • Europe as a war. Europe is one roll per war: Germany wins in 1.4% of them, a number from our own WW2 simulation, not from history.
  • Commanders as people. Yamamoto, Nagumo, Nimitz, Halsey and Kurita aren't in it by name. Codebreaking, scouting and luck are odds.
  • Single ships and land battles. Carriers count in fleet equivalents; battleships and cruisers don't fight on their own. The island campaigns are steps with a cost in American dead.
  • Japan's dead, and the people Japan occupied. The model counts American dead only. The forced labour, the massacres and the famines of the occupation are not in it.

Four review roundsand what they changed

Before the film's numbers were run, the model went through four review rounds, each with a review of the history and a review of the code against the sources, done as AI-assisted review passes. Each pass was told to find what's wrong; every fix went into the code, and each round added tests.

  1. Round 1. Strike north became a war of its own, with its own ending. The November 1941 truce and Hitler's declaration of war became choices. America's talks were tied to strain. The economy was rebuilt from the Strategic Bombing Survey: shipping sunk by submarines, aircraft and mines; Japan's output following its shipping.
  2. Round 2. A win now needs a peace every great power signs, with Britain down too. The dice were keyed so that changing one thing changes only what it causes. A new test suite: every input has to move something.
  3. Round 3. The history reviewer found that a cap on the talks made "America's will decides it" circular: the cap became a stated, tested mechanic. A ceasefire in Siberia under the embargo sends Japan back to its choice. The 1942 battles are fought with the forces really committed. American dead are counted every quarter at war.
  4. Round 4. The history reviewer showed that the restart table only falls: the big step is attacking Americans at all, and that's a judgment from the polls. The factories decide whether America has won by 1948. The film was rewritten around both. The invasion decision is now taken each quarter, and a code fix covers the Soviet Far East under the embargo.

Then the model was locked (version 5, 9 November 2026). The headline held across seeds 19411207, 1, 2, 3, 250,000 wars each: wins 0.21-0.23%, a pre-war empire 1.25-1.30%, surrender 92.5-92.6%. The film's run used the locked files, whose sha256 fingerprints are below.

They're our judgment, written down with what informed each one: the US Strategic Bombing Survey, the Navy's loss tables, the official histories, the polls of the time. No source gives these numbers and nobody can measure them. Every one is listed, with why. Think one is wrong? Change it in the simulator and run it.

Why does Japan win so rarely?

Because a win needs a peace, and a peace needs America to agree to stop. After Pearl Harbor the polls never showed that. Japan is still at war at the end of 1947, often holding much of what it took, in 0.53% of the film's wars; that's not counted as a win or a draw.

What is "a draw or better"?

A win, or a peace that hands back the 1941–42 conquests but leaves Japan its pre-war empire: Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria. 1.52% of the film's wars.

My run doesn't match the film exactly. Why?

A different seed changes the dice, and fewer wars is a smaller sample. The film's settings with seed 19411207 and 1,000,000 wars always give its exact numbers, and every button under Start from and What if loads the seed and number of wars the film used. Small differences between samples are noise, especially for rare outcomes like a win.

What does "start from" do?

It replays real history up to that date exactly as it happened, then lets the model run free. It's how the film shows Japan's chance at each turning point.

Why is it war 4,488 and not 4,487?

War numbers on this page start at 1. In the code the first war is number 0, so the film's war 4,488 is index 4,487 there: war(oddsFor('mid'), rng(warSeed(19411207, 4487))).

Does the page send my settings anywhere?

No. The wars run in your browser, and your odds stay there. The page counts visits and each press of Run, with the name of the what-if button if you used one, using GoatCounter, which uses no cookies and collects no personal data. It never counts your odds, seed or results, and it strips the settings from the address before counting a visit. Its fonts load from Google Fonts, which sees an ordinary web request.

Is it a forecast?

No. It's a way to see what follows from the odds, and the odds are our judgment.
